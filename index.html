1.2 Business Entities
Sole Trader

One person owns and runs the business. Unlimited liability.

Advantages
  • Full control over decisions
  • Keep all profits
  • Simple to set up, low cost
  • Flexibility, quick decisions
Disadvantages
  • Unlimited liability (personal assets at risk)
  • Limited capital, hard to grow
  • No cover when owner ill/holiday
  • Skills limited to one person
Applied Example

A local shawarma stall in Dubai, owner takes all profit but also bears all debt. If sued, personal savings and home are at risk.

Partnership

2 to 20 partners share ownership. Usually unlimited liability unless LLP.

Advantages
  • Shared capital and workload
  • Complementary skills
  • Shared risk
  • Simple formation
Disadvantages
  • Unlimited liability
  • Disagreements between partners
  • Profits split
  • Slow decision-making
Applied Example

Deloitte, PwC, McKinsey are all partnerships, partners share ownership, profits, and legally-unlimited liability in many jurisdictions.

Private Limited Company (Ltd / LLC)

Shares sold privately. Shareholders have limited liability. Separate legal identity.

Advantages
  • Limited liability, shareholders only lose their investment
  • Separate legal entity
  • Can raise capital via private share sale
  • More credibility than sole trader
Disadvantages
  • Cannot sell shares publicly
  • More admin/legal requirements
  • Financial accounts must be filed
  • Complex to set up vs sole trader
Applied Example

On Holding AG was a Swiss LLC before its 2021 NYSE IPO. A Dubai café LLC, owners' personal homes are protected if the business fails.

Public Limited Company (PLC)

Shares sold to the public on a stock exchange. Full limited liability.

Advantages
  • Massive capital raising via IPO
  • Limited liability for shareholders
  • Enhanced public profile/credibility
  • Easier for employees to own shares
Disadvantages
  • Loss of control, shareholders vote
  • Short-term shareholder pressure on profits
  • Expensive listing & compliance costs
  • Hostile takeover risk
Applied Example

On Holding AG (ONON) raised ~$750m in its 2021 IPO. Emirates NBD (ENBD) is listed on the Dubai Financial Market, open to any investor.

Cooperative

Owned and run by its members for mutual benefit. Democratic (1 member = 1 vote).

Advantages
  • Democratic, members have equal say
  • Profits shared among members
  • Ethical business model
  • Strong community loyalty
Disadvantages
  • Slow decision-making
  • Difficult to raise large capital
  • Members may lack business expertise
  • Conflicts among members
Applied Example

REI (US outdoor cooperative), members pay $30 lifetime fee, get dividends. Mondragon (Spain), world's largest cooperative with 81,000 worker-owners.

NGO / Non-profit

Exists for social/charitable purposes. Surplus reinvested into mission, not distributed.

Advantages
  • Tax exemptions
  • Strong public trust & brand
  • Grant funding available
  • Motivates values-driven staff
Disadvantages
  • Cannot distribute profit
  • Dependent on donations/grants
  • Governance complexity
  • Hard to attract high-salary talent
Applied Example

Médecins Sans Frontières (MSF), operates in 70+ countries, 100% funded by donations, no shareholders. One Billion Lives Foundation, NGO redistributing technology access.

Corporate Social Responsibility (CSR)

A business's commitment to ethical behaviour, environmental care, and community contribution beyond legal requirements. Can be philanthropic (charitable), strategic (integrated into business model), or greenwashing (superficial).

Applied, Genuine CSR

Patagonia donates 1% of sales to environmental causes, uses recycled materials, and in 2022 transferred ownership to a trust, profit permanently funds climate work. Their "Don't Buy This Jacket" campaign built authentic loyalty.

Applied, Greenwashing

Volkswagen Dieselgate (2015), marketed diesel cars as "clean" while software cheated emissions tests. Fined $14.7bn, brand damaged severely. Contrast with genuine CSR to explain the difference.

Diseconomies of Scale

Beyond optimal scale, average costs can rise as firm grows too large.

Causes
  • Poor communication across large hierarchies
  • Low morale, workers feel lost, alienated
  • Coordination problems between departments
  • Bureaucracy slows decision-making
  • Principal-agent problem (managers act in self-interest)
Applied Example

General Electric (GE) diversified so far it could no longer manage its divisions efficiently, spun off into 3 separate companies by 2024.

1.6 Multinational Corporations (MNCs)

An MNC operates in multiple countries, typically with headquarters in one nation and subsidiaries/manufacturing in others.

Host Country Benefits
  • FDI (Foreign Direct Investment), capital inflows
  • Employment creation
  • Technology transfer and skills development
  • Tax revenue for government
  • Infrastructure investment
Host Country Concerns
  • Profit repatriation, money leaves host country
  • Cultural erosion, local businesses displaced
  • Environmental exploitation
  • Tax avoidance via transfer pricing
  • Low wages, poor working conditions
Applied, Tesla Gigafactory Shanghai

Tesla built Giga Shanghai in 2019, China's largest FDI project at the time. Benefits: 9,000+ local jobs, supply chain development, technology transfer. Concerns: profits repatriated to US, Chinese competitors (BYD, NIO) gained indirect knowledge spillover, environmental impact of battery production.

2.3 Motivation Theories
Taylor's Scientific Management (1911)

Workers are rational economic beings motivated primarily by money. Break tasks into smallest components, find the "one best way" to do each, pay by results (piece-rate).

Strengths
  • Increased productivity in manufacturing
  • Clear targets for workers
  • Still used: Amazon warehouse piece-rate scanning
Criticisms
  • Dehumanising, treats workers as machines
  • Ignores social and psychological needs
  • Monotonous → low morale, high turnover
Applied, Amazon Fulfillment Centers

Workers scan a target of 300+ items/hour, tracked by productivity software. Piece-rate style incentives. High productivity BUT staff turnover ~150% annually, injuries 2× industry average. Taylor's theory works for output, it fails for wellbeing.

Maslow's Hierarchy of Needs (1943)

Five tiers of human needs, satisfied from bottom upward. A lower need must be mostly met before higher needs motivate.

Implications for managers
  • Identify which level employees are currently at
  • Match rewards to unmet needs
  • Physical/safety first, then social/esteem
Criticisms
  • Rigid hierarchy, people need multiple levels simultaneously
  • Cultural differences, collectivist societies value social > esteem
  • Hard to measure which level someone is at
Applied, Ritz-Carlton Hotels

Ritz-Carlton addresses all five layers: Physiological, above-average pay; Safety, permanent contracts, clear HR policies; Social, "Ladies and Gentlemen serving Ladies and Gentlemen" team ethos; Esteem, Employee of the Month, published guest compliments; Self-actualisation, each employee given $2,000/day discretionary budget to solve any guest problem without manager approval. Result: industry-leading retention and Net Promoter Scores.

Herzberg's Two-Factor Theory (1959)

Distinguishes between factors that cause dissatisfaction (hygiene factors) and factors that cause true motivation (motivators). Removing hygiene factors only prevents unhappiness, it doesn't motivate.

Hygiene Factors (Dissatisfiers)

Must be present to avoid dissatisfaction, but don't directly motivate:

  • Salary and pay
  • Job security
  • Working conditions / environment
  • Company policy and administration
  • Relationships with supervisors
Motivators (Satisfiers)

These genuinely increase motivation and job satisfaction:

  • Achievement and recognition
  • Responsibility
  • Advancement / promotion
  • Personal growth
  • The work itself being interesting
Applied, John Lewis Partnership (UK)

John Lewis is a worker-owned partnership where all "Partners" (staff) share annual profits. Hygiene factors: good pay, secure jobs, quality canteen. Motivators: ownership stake creates genuine responsibility and achievement. Result: consistently higher customer satisfaction and lower staff turnover than competitors like M&S.

3.4 Depreciation HL

The systematic allocation of a non-current asset's cost over its useful life. The 2024 syllabus names two methods: straight line and units of production.

Straight-Line Method
Annual Depreciation= Cost − Residual ValueUseful Life (years)

Example: Machine costs $50,000, residual $5,000, life 5 years.
Annual dep. = (50,000−5,000)/5 = $9,000/year

Units of Production Method
Annual Depreciation= Cost − Residual ValueTotal expected units of output × units produced this year

Example: same machine, expected to produce 90,000 units in its life.
Per unit = (50,000 − 5,000) ÷ 90,000 = $0.50. Year 1: 24,000 units × $0.50 = $12,000. Year 2: 20,000 units = $10,000.
Matches the charge to actual use, so it suits machinery and vehicles. Straight line suits assets used evenly (buildings, furniture).

4.3 Sales Forecasting HL

Sales forecasting means predicting a business's future sales (volume or revenue) over a set period, usually by spotting patterns in past sales data. The syllabus assesses the benefits and limitations of sales forecasting (AO3), so you must be able to judge how far a forecast can be trusted. You will understand the limits much better if you know how a time-series forecast is built, so the method is shown below too.

The four components of a time series
ComponentMeaningExample
TrendThe underlying long-term direction of sales (rising, falling or flat) once short-term ups and downs are smoothed outGlobal EV sales rising year on year since 2020
Seasonal variationRegular, predictable changes within a year linked to seasons, weather, holidays or festivalsToy sales peak in Q4 (Christmas); UAE retail peaks around Ramadan, Eid and the Dubai Shopping Festival
Cyclical variationChanges linked to the business (economic) cycle of boom, downturn, recession and recovery, lasting several yearsCar and housing sales fell sharply in the 2008 to 09 recession
Random variationIrregular, unpredictable one-off events that cannot be forecastCOVID-19 lockdowns (2020), a viral TikTok trend, a factory fire
Forecasting methods

Quantitative (time-series analysis), based on numerical past data:

  • Moving averages smooth out seasonal and random fluctuations to reveal the trend
  • Extrapolation extends the trend line forward into the future (by eye on a graph, or by the average change per period)
  • The forecast is then adjusted by the average seasonal variation for that period

Qualitative, based on judgement. These are used when there is little past data, for example for a new product:

  • Market research (surveys, focus groups, test marketing)
  • Expert opinion and the sales force's own estimates
  • Delphi method: a panel of experts revise their forecasts over several rounds until they reach a consensus

  1. Moving average. Add four consecutive quarters and divide by 4. Y1 Q1 to Q4: (40 + 60 + 80 + 52) ÷ 4 = 58. Roll forward one quarter, Y1 Q2 to Y2 Q1: 240 ÷ 4 = 60.
  2. Centre it. A four-part average falls between two quarters (between Q2 and Q3), so average each pair: (58 + 60) ÷ 2 = 59. This is the trend value for Y1 Q3. (A three-part average needs no centring because it lands on the middle period.)
  3. Seasonal variation = actual − trend. Y1 Q3: 80 − 59 = +21, so Q3 is a strong quarter.

  6. Forecast = extrapolated trend + average seasonal variation. Y4 Q1 = 79 + (−15) = $64k.

The moving average strips out the seasonal zig-zag to reveal a steady upward trend. Extending that trend and then subtracting Q1's typical seasonal dip gives the forecast. Notice the method assumes the pattern of the last three years carries on, which is exactly where its limitations come from.

Benefits of sales forecasting
  • Better planning across the business: operations (output and stock levels), HR (workforce planning, seasonal hiring), finance (cash flow forecasts and budgets) and marketing (timing of campaigns)
  • Better stock control: lowers the risk of stockouts or costly excess stock, which supports JIT (see 5.6)
  • Improves cash flow management: expected revenue feeds directly into the cash flow forecast (3.7)
  • Helps raise finance: banks and investors expect sales forecasts in a business plan
  • Sets targets against which actual performance can be measured (variance analysis)
  • Reveals seasonal patterns so promotions, staffing and production can be planned in advance
Limitations of sales forecasting
  • Based on past data: assumes the future will follow past patterns, which is often untrue in dynamic markets
  • Random variations cannot be predicted: pandemics, natural disasters and viral trends make forecasts wrong overnight
  • Less accurate the further ahead you forecast; not possible for new products with no sales history
  • Ignores qualitative factors such as competitor actions, changing tastes, new regulations and economic shocks
  • Time-consuming and costly to collect good data and analyse it, especially for small businesses
  • Risk of bias: over-optimistic managers may inflate forecasts, and people may treat a forecast as fact
Applied, COVID-19 (random variation)

In March 2020, panic buying sent demand for toilet paper and hand sanitiser soaring while airline demand collapsed. No forecast built on 2015 to 2019 data could have predicted this. It is the clearest example of why random variation limits time-series forecasting.

Applied, UAE retail (seasonal variation)

Dubai retailers plan stock and staffing around predictable peaks such as Ramadan and Eid, the Dubai Shopping Festival and back-to-school. Note the twist: Ramadan follows the lunar calendar and moves about 11 days earlier every year, so the quarter it falls in shifts over time. A seasonal variation calculated from old data slowly becomes inaccurate.

Applied, Zara (reducing reliance on forecasts)

Zara produces in small batches and uses daily store sales data to decide what to make next, rather than committing to large orders months ahead. It reduces the cost of forecasting errors (unsold stock, markdowns) instead of trying to forecast perfectly. This links forecasting to lean production (5.3).

Evaluation tip
How reliable a forecast is depends on: how stable the market is (mature FMCG versus tech), how far ahead it looks, how much reliable past data exists, and whether it is combined with qualitative methods. A strong conclusion says a forecast is a planning guide, not a guarantee, so firms should update it regularly and have contingency plans (5.7).

5.2 Operations Methods

An operations method is the way a business organises production. The syllabus names four: job, batch, mass/flow and mass customisation. The right choice depends mainly on the volume of output and the variety customers want.

MethodDescriptionAdvantagesDisadvantagesExample
Job productionA single, unique item made to an individual customer's specification, one at a timeHigh quality, fully customised; premium prices; motivating, skilled workLabour-intensive and slow; high unit cost; no economies of scaleBurj Khalifa (construction), tailored suits, wedding cakes
Batch productionGroups (batches) of identical products are made together; each batch completes a stage before moving to the nextSome economies of scale; flexible, since the firm can switch between product variants; lower unit cost than jobDowntime between batches (cleaning, resetting machines); work-in-progress stock; repetitive tasks; needs careful schedulingBakeries (croissants, then baguettes), clothing in sizes, medicines

Factors that influence the choice of method
  • Size and stability of demand: flow production only pays off with large, predictable demand
  • Nature of the product: bespoke (job) versus standardised (flow)
  • Finance available: flow and mass customisation need heavy capital investment
  • Technology: automation and CAD/CAM make mass customisation possible
  • Business objectives and positioning: premium quality versus cost leadership
  • Labour skills: job production needs highly skilled craftspeople

Many firms combine methods, for example batch-producing components and then flow-producing the final assembly. Firms also change method as they grow: a start-up bakery may move from job to batch to flow.

Applied, Nike By You (mass customisation)

Nike members design their own trainers online by choosing colours, materials and custom text. Pairs are built to order and take roughly 3 to 4 weeks to arrive. Nike charges a premium over the standard shoe but still uses standard lasts and components, so it keeps economies of scale. The trade-off for the customer is slower delivery.

Applied, Dell (build-to-order)

Dell built its business on letting customers configure PCs online and assembling each order from standard components. Because it made only what had been ordered and paid for, it held very little finished-goods stock and freed up working capital. This shows mass customisation working alongside a lean operation.

5.3 Lean Production & Quality Management HL

Lean production is an approach to operations that aims to minimise waste of all resources (materials, time, labour, space and energy) while maintaining or improving quality. It is "doing more with less". It grew out of the Toyota Production System developed by Taiichi Ohno in post-war Japan. The two features in the syllabus are less waste and greater efficiency.

Methods of lean production
Continuous improvement (kaizen)

Kaizen ("change for the better") is a philosophy of making small, ongoing, incremental improvements suggested by all employees, from the shop floor to senior managers, rather than relying on occasional big changes.

Pros
  • Cheap: many small ideas, little capital
  • Uses the knowledge of the workers who do the job
  • Empowering and motivating (links to Herzberg and Pink)
  • Gains add up over time
Cons
  • Needs a supportive culture and trust
  • Results are slow; diminishing returns over time
  • Not enough when radical change is needed
  • Staff may resist or run out of ideas
Just-in-time (JIT)

Raw materials and components arrive exactly when they are needed for production, and finished goods are made only when ordered. Little or no buffer stock is held. Stock is often "pulled" through the factory using kanban signals (cards or electronic signals that trigger the next order).

Pros
  • Lower storage and insurance costs
  • Less cash tied up in stock, so better working capital
  • Less waste, damage and obsolescence
  • Quality problems show up immediately
Cons
  • Very vulnerable to supply disruption
  • Relies on highly reliable suppliers
  • Bulk-buying discounts are lost
  • Hard to meet sudden surges in demand

JIT is compared with just-in-case (JIC) in 5.6.

Cradle to cradle (C2C) design and manufacturing

Cradle to cradle (William McDonough and Michael Braungart, 2002) designs products so that at the end of their life every material is reused, so there is no "waste". It replaces the linear cradle to grave model (take, make, dispose).

  • Biological nutrients: materials that safely biodegrade and return to nature
  • Technical nutrients: materials such as metals and some plastics that are recycled again and again in a closed loop without losing quality
  • Products are designed for disassembly, use safe and non-toxic materials, and are made using renewable energy
  • Closely linked to circular business models and the sustainability key concept
Benefits
  • Lower raw material costs in the long run
  • Stronger CSR image and brand loyalty
  • Gets ahead of tightening environmental laws
  • Supports the triple bottom line
Drawbacks
  • High design, R&D and certification costs
  • Needs take-back and recovery logistics
  • May raise prices
  • Relies on customers returning products
Applied, Herman Miller and Interface

Herman Miller designed its Mirra office chair for C2C certification, so it comes apart easily for recycling. Carpet-tile maker Interface runs a take-back programme (ReEntry) that recovers old tiles so their materials can be reused.

Methods of managing quality
Quality circles

Small groups of workers from the same area meet regularly to identify, analyse and solve quality and production problems, then present solutions to management. They began in 1960s Japan (Kaoru Ishikawa).

  • + Uses shop-floor knowledge; motivating; low cost
  • − Takes time away from production; managers may ignore ideas; needs a participative culture
Benchmarking

Comparing the firm's products, processes or performance with the best in the industry, or best practice in any industry, to find and copy improvements. The steps are: choose the area, choose the benchmark partner, collect data, analyse the gap, implement changes, review.

  • + Realistic targets; learns from proven practice
  • − Data can be hard to get; copying is not innovating; may not fit the firm's culture
Total quality management (TQM)

A culture in which every employee at every level is responsible for quality all the time. It focuses on meeting the needs of internal and external customers, with a goal of zero defects and continuous improvement.

  • + Lower waste costs; stronger reputation; motivated staff
  • − Expensive training; slow culture change; risk of bureaucracy and resistance
Applied, Great Ormond Street Hospital (benchmarking outside the industry)

The London children's hospital studied Formula 1 pit-stop crews to redesign how patients are handed over from surgery to intensive care. It introduced clear roles, a set sequence and checklists, and reported fewer handover errors. This is benchmarking best practice from a completely different industry.

Impact of lean production and TQM on an organisation
Positive impacts
  • Costs: less waste, rework and storage, so lower unit costs and better margins
  • Quality and reputation: fewer defects, more customer loyalty, and grounds for premium pricing
  • Workforce: empowerment, teamwork and multi-skilling raise motivation
  • Cash flow: less money tied up in stock
  • Sustainability: lower resource use supports the triple bottom line
Negative impacts / challenges
  • Upfront cost: training, new systems and reorganisation
  • Culture change: needs commitment from top to bottom and can face resistance (links to 2.5)
  • Stress: constant improvement and zero-defect targets add pressure
  • Supply risk: lean (JIT) supply chains break easily in disruptions
  • Job losses: efficiency gains may mean redundancies, which hurts morale

Applied, Zara (lean fashion)

Zara makes much of its fashion range in small batches at factories close to its Spanish headquarters, so new designs can reach stores within weeks. Less overproduction means fewer end-of-season markdowns than rivals who commit to large orders months ahead.

Drivers include rising wages in China, supply chain shocks (COVID-19, the 2021 Suez Canal blockage), tariffs and trade wars, automation reducing the labour-cost advantage, government subsidies, and the lower carbon footprint of shorter supply chains.

The break-even point (BEP) is the output level where total revenue = total costs, i.e. profit is zero. Below BEP the firm makes a loss, above it makes a profit.

  • Assumes costs/price are linear (ignores discounts, economies of scale)
  • Fixed costs may change at higher output (stepped costs)
  • Only suitable for single-product analysis
  • Static, ignores market changes over time
  • Ignores qualitative factors and demand: it shows costs at each output, not how many units customers will actually buy at that price
  • Only as reliable as the cost and revenue data used
5.6 Production Planning HL

Production planning makes sure the right inputs are in the right place at the right time, at the lowest cost. It covers the supply chain, stock control, and measuring how efficiently resources are used. Paper 2 often asks you to calculate these metrics and then decide (make or buy, JIT or JIC).

The supply chain process (local and global)

A supply chain is the network of organisations, people, activities and resources involved in getting a product from raw materials to the final consumer. Supply chain management coordinates every stage so that cost, speed and quality are optimised.

Measuring efficiency: capacity, defects and productivity

Worked example: a furniture maker can produce up to 10,000 chairs a month. This month it made 8,000 chairs, 240 of them defective, using 40 workers × 160 hours (6,400 labour hours) and 10 machines × 200 hours (2,000 machine hours).

Interpreting operating leverage
Operating leverage shows how sensitive profit is to a change in sales. It is high when fixed costs are large compared with variable costs (airlines, car makers, software firms). Profits then grow fast in booms but collapse in downturns, so the firm is riskier. It is low when most costs are variable. Operating leverage is very high just above break-even and falls as output grows.
Cost to buy (CTB) versus cost to make (CTM)
CTB=P × QP = supplier's price per unit
CTM=TFC + (AVC × Q)

Worked example: a firm needs 5,000 components. A supplier charges $8 each: CTB = 8 × 5,000 = $40,000. Making them in-house costs fixed costs of $15,000 plus $4 per unit: CTM = 15,000 + (4 × 5,000) = $35,000. Making saves $5,000.

Break-even quantity: 8Q = 15,000 + 4Q, so Q = 3,750. Below 3,750 units buying is cheaper (no fixed costs); above it making is cheaper.

Qualitative reasons to MAKE
  • Full control over quality and delivery times
  • Protects secret designs, recipes and IP
  • Uses spare capacity (fixed costs are already paid)
  • Not dependent on a supplier who may raise prices or fail
  • The activity is a core competency or a source of competitive advantage
Qualitative reasons to BUY
  • Supplier has specialist expertise and economies of scale
  • No capital investment or extra staff needed
  • Flexibility to change volumes or suppliers
  • Management can focus on core activities
  • Quicker to start: no set-up time

The make-or-buy decision links directly to outsourcing and insourcing (5.4). A quantitative CTB/CTM answer should always be weighed against these qualitative factors.

5.7 Crisis Management & Contingency Planning HL

A crisis is an unexpected event that threatens serious harm to a business, its stakeholders, its reputation or even its survival, and needs urgent action. Examples include product recalls, cyberattacks and IT failures, natural disasters, pandemics, supply chain collapse, industrial accidents and scandals.

Factors that affect effective crisis management
1 · Transparency

Being open and honest about what happened, why, and what is being done. Hiding information destroys trust when it comes out, and it usually does come out.

Poor transparency, Boeing 737 MAX

Pilots were not fully told about the MCAS flight-control software. After two crashes (Lion Air, 2018; Ethiopian Airlines, 2019) killed 346 people, the aircraft was grounded worldwide for about 20 months. The damage to Boeing's reputation and finances lasted for years.

2 · Communication

Clear, consistent, timely messages to all stakeholders (customers, employees, investors, media, government) through suitable channels, ideally from one trusted spokesperson, and showing empathy.

Good communication, KFC UK (2018)

After switching its delivery contract to DHL, KFC ran out of chicken and had to shut hundreds of its roughly 900 UK restaurants. It apologised with humour in a full-page "FCK, we're sorry" advert (an empty bucket with the letters rearranged) and kept customers updated on which stores were open. The ad won a Gold Lion at Cannes and turned a supply failure into a PR success.

3 · Speed

Acting quickly to contain the crisis before it escalates. Delay looks like indifference and lets rumours spread, and on social media that can happen in hours. Speed must not come at the cost of accuracy, though.

Fast action, Johnson & Johnson Tylenol (1982)

After seven people died from Tylenol capsules that had been laced with cyanide, J&J warned the public and recalled about 31 million bottles nationwide within a week, at a cost of around $100m. It then relaunched with tamper-evident packaging. This is still the textbook example of putting customer safety first.

4 · Control

Management takes charge of the situation and the narrative. That means a crisis team with clear roles and authority, controlling the flow of information, physically containing the problem (e.g. a recall), and steering operations back to normal.

Loss of control, Delta and CrowdStrike (2024)

A faulty CrowdStrike software update crashed Windows systems worldwide on 19 July 2024. Most airlines recovered within a day or two, but Delta's crew-tracking system could not cope. It cancelled over 7,000 flights in five days, affecting about 1.3 million passengers, at a cost of roughly $550m.

Toyota's post-2011 business continuity plan (chip stockpiles held by suppliers, multiple suppliers for the same part, and a database mapping its suppliers' suppliers) let it react far faster than rivals when the 2021 chip shortage hit. It is a clear example of upfront cost buying lower risk and saving time.

Evaluation tip
Contingency planning is most worthwhile when a crisis is likely or its impact would be severe: airlines, banks, food producers, chemical plants, hospitals. Small firms may lack the money and time, so they should prioritise their biggest risks. A strong conclusion: contingency planning cannot prevent crises, but it greatly improves the quality of crisis management when one strikes.

4.3 Sales forecasting HL
Method in brief

A time series has a trend, seasonal, cyclical and random variation. Moving averages smooth the data to reveal the trend, which is then extrapolated and adjusted by the average seasonal variation.

Three-part moving average on monthly sales 20, 26, 23, 29, 32: (20 + 26 + 23) ÷ 3 = 23, then 26, then 28. The trend is rising.
Four-part averages fall between two periods, so they must be centred (average each pair).

The lowest average cost (the most efficient scale) is around 10,000 units. Growing beyond it raises unit costs.

Internal vs external growth

Internal (organic): new products, new markets, more capacity, funded by retained profit. Slower, lower risk, keeps control and culture.
External: joining with other firms. Faster, buys market share and capability, but costly and risks culture clashes.

Why grow, or stay small?

Grow: economies of scale, market share and power, survival, diversification of risk, owners' ambition.
Stay small: owner control, personal service, niche markets, lower risk, limited finance, avoiding diseconomies.

External growth methods
  • Merger: two firms agree to combine
  • Acquisition / takeover: buying a controlling stake (hostile if unwanted)
  • Joint venture: a new, jointly owned business
  • Strategic alliance: cooperation with no new entity
  • Franchising: selling the right to use the brand and system
Case, acquisition

Microsoft completed its roughly $69bn takeover of Activision Blizzard (Call of Duty) in October 2023, after competition regulators in the UK, US and EU scrutinised it. Fast growth in gaming content, but regulators were concerned about market power.

Case, joint venture

Sony Honda Mobility (set up 2022) combines Sony's electronics and software with Honda's car-making to build EVs under the Afeela brand. They share costs and risks without either firm buying the other.

Case, franchising

McDonald's operates around 95% of its restaurants through franchisees. It grows quickly with franchisees' money while earning rent and royalties, but has less day-to-day control over quality.

Definition Sheet
Key terms for 2-mark "define" questions · grouped by unit · use precise IB wording
Unit 1 · Business Organization
Business
An organisation that combines human, physical and financial resources to produce goods or services to satisfy customer needs.
Entrepreneur
An individual who plans, organises and takes the financial risk of running a business in pursuit of profit.
Limited liability
Shareholders are only liable for the debts of the business up to the amount they invested; personal assets are protected.
Unlimited liability
Owners are personally responsible for all business debts, risking personal assets.
Stakeholder
Any individual or group with an interest in, or affected by, the activities and decisions of a business.
CSR
Corporate Social Responsibility, a business's obligation to act ethically and consider its social and environmental impact beyond legal requirements.
Economies of scale
Cost advantages a business gains as output increases, reducing average cost per unit.
Multinational corporation (MNC)
A business that operates production or service facilities in more than one country.
Merger
When two businesses agree to combine and form one new legal entity.
Acquisition
When one business buys a controlling interest (over 50%) in another business.
Unit 2 · Human Resources
Leadership
The ability to influence, inspire and direct people towards achieving organisational goals.
Motivation
The internal and external factors that drive an individual to take action and put effort into their work.
Labour turnover
The percentage of the workforce that leaves a business over a given period, usually one year.
Appraisal
The formal assessment of an employee's performance against set objectives.
Job enrichment
Giving employees more challenging and meaningful tasks with greater responsibility (vertical loading).
Job enlargement
Increasing the number of tasks an employee performs at the same level (horizontal loading).
Empowerment
Giving employees the authority and autonomy to make decisions about their own work.
Span of control
The number of subordinates directly reporting to a manager.
Delayering
Removing one or more levels of hierarchy from an organisational structure.
Unit 3 · Finance & Accounts
Gross profit
The profit made from trading, calculated as sales revenue minus cost of goods sold.
Net profit
The profit remaining after all expenses, interest and tax have been deducted from gross profit.
Liquidity
The ability of a business to pay its short-term debts as they fall due.
Working capital
The funds available for the day-to-day running of a business; current assets minus current liabilities.
Depreciation
The reduction in the value of a non-current asset over its useful life.
Insolvency
When a business cannot pay its debts as they fall due (a cash flow problem).
Bankruptcy
A legal status when a business or individual cannot repay debts and assets are liquidated.
Investment appraisal
The quantitative techniques used to assess the financial viability of a capital investment decision.
Budget
A quantitative financial plan for a future period, setting targets for revenue and expenditure.
Variance
The difference between a budgeted figure and the actual figure achieved.
Unit 4 · Marketing
Marketing
The management process of identifying, anticipating and satisfying customer needs profitably.
Market share
The proportion of total sales in a market held by one business or product, expressed as a percentage.
Market orientation
A business approach that focuses on identifying and meeting customer needs through market research.
Product orientation
A business approach focused on the product itself and its quality, assuming demand will follow.
USP
Unique Selling Point, a feature that distinguishes a product from competitors.
Product life cycle
The stages a product passes through from launch to withdrawal: introduction, growth, maturity, decline.
Penetration pricing
Setting a low initial price to quickly gain market share.
Price skimming
Setting a high initial price on a new product, lowering it over time.
Marketing mix
The combination of elements (7Ps) used to market a product effectively.
Sales forecasting HL
Predicting a business's future sales over a given period, usually by analysing patterns in past sales data.
Trend HL
The underlying long-term direction of a data series once short-term fluctuations are removed.
Seasonal variation HL
Regular, predictable changes in sales within a year, linked to seasons, weather or holidays.
Cyclical variation HL
Fluctuations in sales linked to the business cycle (boom, recession, recovery) over several years.
Random variation HL
Irregular, unpredictable changes in sales caused by one-off events.
Moving average HL
An average of a set number of consecutive periods, recalculated as each new period is added, used to smooth data and reveal the trend.
Extrapolation HL
Extending a trend line beyond the existing data to forecast future values.
Unit 5 · Operations
Break-even point
The level of output where total revenue equals total costs, so neither profit nor loss is made.
Contribution
The amount each unit sold contributes to covering fixed costs, after variable costs are paid.
Margin of safety
The amount by which actual output exceeds the break-even output.
Fixed costs
Costs that do not change with the level of output (e.g. rent, salaries).
Variable costs
Costs that change directly with the level of output (e.g. raw materials).
Capacity utilisation
The proportion of maximum possible output that a business is currently producing.
Target price
The price a firm must charge to reach a target profit at a given level of output.
Job production
Making a single, unique product to an individual customer's specification.
Batch production
Making a set quantity of identical products together, each batch completing one stage before moving to the next.
Mass / flow production
Continuous production of large volumes of standardised products along a production line.
Mass customisation
Using flexible, technology-driven production to tailor products to individual customers at close to mass-production unit cost.
Outsourcing / subcontracting
Transferring a business activity to an external provider (third party) instead of doing it in-house.
Offshoring
Relocating business activities to another country, whether done by the firm itself or by a third party.
Insourcing
Bringing a previously outsourced activity back in-house to be done by the firm's own employees.
Reshoring
Returning previously offshored business activities to the home country.
Lean production HL
An approach to operations that minimises waste of all resources while maintaining or improving quality.
Kaizen HL
Continuous improvement through small, incremental changes suggested by all employees.
Just-in-time (JIT) HL
A stock control system in which inputs arrive only when needed for production, so little or no buffer stock is held.
Just-in-case (JIC) HL
Holding large buffer stocks to protect against supply delays or unexpected rises in demand.
Cradle to cradle HL
Designing products so that all materials can be safely returned to nature or recycled indefinitely, eliminating waste.
Quality control HL
Detecting defects by inspecting products, mainly at the end of production.
Quality assurance HL
Preventing defects by building quality into every stage of production, with all employees responsible.
Quality circle HL
A small group of workers who meet regularly to identify and solve quality and production problems.
Benchmarking HL
Comparing a firm's products, processes or performance with industry best practice to identify improvements.
Total quality management (TQM) HL
A culture in which every employee is responsible for continuously improving quality to meet customer needs, aiming for zero defects.
Supply chain HL
The network of organisations, activities and resources involved in moving a product from raw materials to the final consumer.
Buffer stock HL
The minimum level of stock held as a safety margin against delays or unexpected demand.
Lead time HL
The time between placing an order for stock and receiving it.
Reorder level HL
The stock level at which a new order is placed.
Reorder quantity HL
The amount of stock ordered each time a new order is placed.
Defect rate HL
The percentage of total output that is faulty.
Labour productivity HL
Output per unit of labour input, e.g. per worker or per labour hour.
Capital productivity HL
Output per unit of capital input, e.g. per machine hour.
Operating leverage HL
The sensitivity of profit to changes in sales, determined by the proportion of fixed costs in total costs.
Cost to buy / cost to make HL
The cost of buying units from a supplier (P × Q) compared with producing them in-house (TFC + AVC × Q).
Crisis management HL
The actions taken to deal with an unexpected, threatening event as it happens, to limit damage and restore normal operations.
Contingency planning HL
Planning in advance for unexpected events by identifying possible crises and preparing responses to them.
